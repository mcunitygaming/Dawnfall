## I. Narrative Hierarchy (Importance in the Story)

This defines _how central a character is to the narrative_, not what role they play.

### 1. Protagonist

The main character whose goals, choices, and journey drive the story forward. The story is primarily framed through their perspective or struggle.

- The audience follows their objective
    
- Central to the plot’s emotional and narrative arc
    
- May not always be “good,” but is the story’s focus
    

### 2. Deuteragonist

The second most important character in the story.

- Has significant narrative weight and development
    
- Often has their own subplot or parallel arc
    
- Closely tied to the protagonist’s journey (supporting or contrasting)
    

_Note: A deuteragonist can fulfill any narrative function (mentor, love interest, rival, etc.). Importance ≠ role._

### 3. Tritagonist (Optional Tier)

The third most important character.

- May act as mediator, secondary driver, or alternate perspective
    
- Often balances or complicates the protagonist–deuteragonist dynamic
    

### 4. Tertiary Characters

Minor characters who exist to support the story world or specific scenes.

- Limited development and screen time
    
- Serve narrow functions (exposition, realism, obstacles, atmosphere)
    
- Rarely have independent arcs

## II. Narrative Functions (What a Character Does)

These describe a character’s _purpose in the story_, regardless of their importance level.

### 1. Confidant / Sidekick

A trusted companion who supports the protagonist emotionally, physically, or strategically.

- Offers advice, loyalty, or comic relief
    
- Often present in key scenes
    
- Can challenge or reinforce protagonist decisions
    

### 2. Mentor

A guiding figure who provides knowledge, training, or wisdom.

- Helps shape protagonist’s skills or worldview
    
- Often associated with early-stage growth
    
- May be temporary or long-term
    

### 3. Love Interest

A character tied to the protagonist through romantic or emotional attachment.

- Motivates emotional stakes or personal growth
    
- Can support, challenge, or complicate the protagonist’s goals
    
- Not always a “reward”—can be independent and complex
    

### 4. Foil

A character designed to contrast another (usually the protagonist).

- Highlights traits through opposition (values, methods, personality)
    
- Can be ally or enemy
    
- Works best through direct comparison
    

### 5. Antagonist

A character (or force) that opposes the protagonist’s goal.

- Creates conflict and obstacles
    
- Can be moral, ideological, emotional, or physical opposition
    
- Does not need to be “evil”
    

### 6. Shapeshifter (Dynamic Role)

A character whose alignment, loyalty, or identity changes significantly.

- Introduces uncertainty and tension
    
- Can shift between ally and enemy
    
- Often tied to betrayal, growth, or hidden truth reveals
